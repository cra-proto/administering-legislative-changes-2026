The Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) is responsible for administering legislation related to tax and benefits. To administer and enforce new tax measures (including amendments to existing tax measures), the CRA usually requires enacted legislation.

In certain circumstances, the CRA can consider administering a new tax measure on the basis of proposed legislative amendments where there is a tabled Notice of Ways and Means Motion (NWMM). Administering in this context generally means permitting taxpayers to file and be initially assessed in accordance with proposed legislation. This longstanding practice contributes to a stable tax system that balances the needs of taxpayers with those of the Government.

The CRA follows the steps outlined below, on a case-by-case basis to examine proposed tax measures and determine if they meet the parameters of the CRA's approach to administering proposed legislation:

  1. The CRA determines whether it can consider administering the measure

    There are two key factors that the CRA considers when determining whether it can administer a proposed measure:

    • Proposed legislation is sufficiently detailed for the CRA to administer and is in its final form before enactment, with only potential amendments from the House of Commons or Senate remaining
    • The Government's clear, public intention to proceed with the legislative amendments through the Parliamentary process (for example, tabling a NWMM)

    The CRA does not administer draft legislative proposals

    The CRA does not administer draft legislative proposals that are released for consultation as they are not considered final and changes could be made as a result of the feedback received. Administering draft proposals in advance of consultations being finalized would also undermine the consultation process.

    Administration ceases if the Government no longer intends to proceed

    If the Government announces that it no longer intends to proceed with a proposed legislative amendment, the second key factor would no longer be met, and the CRA would cease to administer that proposed amendment.

    Situations where the CRA cannot administer on the basis of proposed legislation

    In some cases, Royal Assent of the relevant legislation is required prior to administering a new tax measure.

    Where proposed legislation results in an increase to refundable credits or benefits to the taxpayer, or a significant rebate or refund, the CRA will wait until the legislation for that specific measure has received Royal Assent before making these types of payments. This is because there is no authority for the CRA to make these increased payments out of the Consolidated Revenue Fund (the Government of Canada's account for public funds) until the legislation has received Royal Assent.

    Examples of increased payments changes the CRA cannot administer

    Changes to the Canada Child Benefit, Canada Workers Benefit, Canada Groceries and Essentials Benefit or GST/HST rebates would typically require Royal Assent.

    Non-refundable tax credits are treated differently from refundable credits. The CRA has generally administered non-refundable tax credits on the basis of proposed legislation. This is because a non-refundable tax credit or a deduction would not, by itself, result in a refund, although it may result in reduced taxes being paid by the taxpayer.

    Examples of non-refundable tax credits the CRA can administer

    Changes to the tax rate for a personal income tax bracket would not necessarily require Royal Assent, since it would be a reduction (or increase) to tax owing, rather than a refundable credit.

    Finally, proposed legislation related to penalties, taxpayer information sharing rules and criminal provisions usually comes into effect only upon Royal Assent, and as such is not administered until then.

  2. The CRA assesses whether it is able to administer the measure

    Where a proposed tax measure meets the parameters described in Step 1, the CRA must also consider a number of internal factors before agreeing to administer the proposed measure. For example, the CRA must consider its:

    • Operational capacity
    • Funding
    • Readiness of forms, guidance and system changes

    Each of these factors will impact the CRA's capacity to implement the administration of a new measure at a specific time.

  3. The CRA advises taxpayers that it is administering proposed legislation

    Where the CRA decides to administer a proposed measure, it notifies taxpayers through banners on relevant CRA webpages, instructions in specific forms, releasing new guidance or updating existing guides. Banners posted on the webpage related to a specific measure are usually the first to be updated. Taxpayers are encouraged to consult these pages, along with the relevant forms and guides when available, for the most current information on the CRA's approach to administering specific measures.

Filing based on proposed legislation

The CRA's longstanding practice allows taxpayers to voluntarily file on the basis of proposed legislation once the related form, guide and system updates are completed.

This practice provides consistency and fairness in the treatment of taxpayers. Choosing to file based on proposed legislation can result in greater certainty for taxpayers and ease compliance burden by ensuring the need to file returns only once. It also reduces the possibility of interest accumulation when changes are eventually implemented.

The CRA does not enforce compliance with proposed legislation until it becomes law. As such, the CRA will apply the existing law, rather than the proposed legislation, in any objection or appeals process.

More information

While this page provides a general overview of the CRA's practice of administering proposed legislation, taxpayers are encouraged to visit the relevant CRA webpages, and consult the appropriate forms and guides, for the most current information on a specific tax measure.
